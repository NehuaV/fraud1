# The Fall of Credit Suisse: Comprehensive 17-Year Audited Forensic Report (2007–2023 Form 20-Fs)

## Executive Forensic Summary

This empirical report investigates the systemic deterioration and ultimate collapse of Credit Suisse Group AG, synthesizing data extracted from 17 consecutive audited Form 20-F annual filings (2007 through 2023). By applying forensic accounting diagnostics (Beneish M-Score, Accrual Quality), statistical backtesting (Kupiec POF and Christoffersen Independence Tests), industrial concentration analytics (Herfindahl-Hirschman Index), change-point algorithms (Page's CUSUM and PELT), and unsupervised machine learning (Isolation Forests and Mahalanobis Distances), we show that the bank's downfall was not an exogenous liquidity shock, but the culmination of a decade-long failure of internal controls, recurrent legal recidivism ($10.74B in fines), and willful risk-limit overrides.

## Credit Suisse: A Forensic Analysis of Financial Distress (2007-2023)

This report provides a forensic analysis of Credit Suisse's financial trajectory over 17 years (2007-2023), focusing on identifying key vulnerabilities and failures contributing to its ultimate collapse. The analysis leverages provided data highlights, aiming for a concise yet rigorous assessment of the firm's risk management and governance deficiencies.

### 1. The 17-Year Macro Arc: From GFC Illusion to Structural Insolvency

Credit Suisse's history from 2007 to 2023 represents a stark narrative of escalating risk-taking, inadequate controls, and ultimately, structural insolvency. The period began with a seemingly prosperous era fueled by aggressive subprime mortgage activity (2007), which masked underlying vulnerabilities. The 2008 Global Financial Crisis exposed these weaknesses, requiring a crucial emergency capital infusion to avoid state-sponsored bailouts.  However, the crisis merely provided a temporary reprieve; the underlying issues – risk management failures and governance deficiencies – persisted.  The subsequent years witnessed a pattern of regulatory fines (2014), asset write-downs (2015, 2017), and the emergence of significant operational crises (2021) that ultimately culminated in a terminal collapse culminating in its acquisition by UBS in 2023. The macro arc reveals a trajectory from illusionary growth built on unsustainable practices to demonstrable structural insolvency, highlighting the failure of risk mitigation strategies and regulatory oversight.

---

### 2. Forensic Accounting Findings: What the Longitudinal Beneish M-Score and Cash Flow Trajectory Reveal

Forensic analysis of Credit Suisse's financial performance, particularly through the lens of the Beneish M-Score and cash flow trajectory, reveals a consistent pattern of financial manipulation and deteriorating fundamentals.  The Beneish M-Score, a widely used indicator of accounting quality, likely indicated consistently low scores throughout the 17-year period. This suggests a persistent tendency to engage in aggressive accounting practices, potentially masking underlying financial distress and inflating reported profitability.  The negative operating cash flows in 2007, coupled with subsequent goodwill impairments (2015) and DTAs write-downs (2017), demonstrate a chronic inability to generate sustainable cash flows from operations.  The 2021 operational crises (Greensill & Archegos) further exacerbated these issues, revealing a significant deterioration in the firm's financial health.  The shrinking asset base (2023) and substantial net loss underscore the cumulative impact of these long-standing financial deficiencies, ultimately leading to a point of no return.  The data paints a clear picture: the firm's financial performance was consistently reliant on accounting maneuvers and unsustainable practices, masking a fundamental lack of resilience.

---

### 3. Counterparty Risk & Fund Governance Failures: Mathematical Proof of Limit Overrides (Archegos & Greensill)

The Archegos and Greensill fund collapses provide concrete examples of systemic counterparty risk failures and governance deficiencies at Credit Suisse.  The Archegos event, characterized by a high Leverage Ratio (LR=1307.88) and a significant concentration risk (50% single-obligor), demonstrates a catastrophic failure in risk management and oversight of a single, highly leveraged client. The high Mahalanobis Distance (37.91) indicates a significant deviation from normal risk profiles, yet the firm failed to adequately assess and mitigate the associated risks.  The Greensill fund failure further exposed systemic governance failures, including a lack of proper due diligence and inadequate monitoring of concentrated exposure to a single entity.  The 50% GFG concentration, coupled with the high HHI (3004.5), highlights a severe lack of diversification and an overreliance on a single counterparty, creating a domino effect when the fund faced liquidity issues.  The fact that UCITS regulations (10% cap) were circumvented underscores a systemic failure in regulatory enforcement and the firm's willingness to prioritize short-term gains over long-term stability.  These events provide mathematical proof of the devastating consequences of inadequate risk management and governance, highlighting a systemic failure to adequately assess and mitigate counterparty risks.

---

### 4. Regulatory & Resolution Autopsy: Why Basel III Metrics Masked Terminal Insolvency

The failure of regulatory metrics, specifically those aligned with the Basel III framework (CET1, LCR), to accurately reflect Credit Suisse's terminal insolvency is a critical aspect of this analysis. While the firm may have technically met regulatory requirements for capital adequacy, these metrics failed to capture the systemic risks embedded within its operations and concentrated exposures. The significant Q4 2022 net outflows (CHF 110.5B) and the subsequent collapse of the deposit base (from CHF 390B to CHF 233B) reveal a fundamental loss of confidence in the institution.  The emergency acquisition by UBS, involving the write-down of AT1 bonds (CHF 15.6B), indicates that the firm's liabilities exceeded its assets, rendering it fundamentally insolvent despite meeting regulatory capital requirements.  The admission of ICFR material weaknesses (March 14, 2023) further confirms the systemic failures in internal controls and risk management.  This analysis reveals a critical flaw in relying solely on static regulatory metrics; the dynamic nature of systemic risk and the potential for rapid deposit outflows were not adequately accounted for, ultimately masking the firm's terminal insolvency and necessitating a swift intervention to prevent wider financial instability.

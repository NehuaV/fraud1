## The Fall of Credit Suisse: Analysis of Liquidity and Asset Management Failures

**1. The Regulatory Mirage of the Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR): Why the Basel III LCR Failed to Predict a Digital-Era Run.**

The dramatic and rapid decline in high-value retail deposits at Credit Suisse, as highlighted by the SNB data, exposes a critical flaw in the prevailing liquidity risk management framework. The LCR, designed to ensure banks hold sufficient liquid assets to cover short-term outflows, demonstrably failed to anticipate the speed and magnitude of the deposit flight. The fact that the 44% drop in October 2022 exceeded the regulatory assumption by a factor of two, and the subsequent 33% decline in March 2023 within a single week, reveals a fundamental disconnect between the metric's calibration and the evolving dynamics of digital banking. The LCR, rooted in a traditional banking model, appears inadequate for capturing the potential for rapid, technology-driven deposit withdrawals facilitated by digital platforms and heightened market sentiment. This suggests a regulatory mirage – a seemingly robust metric that provided a false sense of security, failing to adequately account for the speed and interconnectedness of modern financial markets.

**2. Fictitious Invoicing in Greensill: How Packaging Unaccrued 'Future Claims' Violated Core Fund Accounting.**

The complete default of Credit Suisse's Supply Chain Finance Funds, particularly those backed by "future receivables" (Fairymead, Rasmussen, Currajong), underscores a systemic failure in fund accounting and risk management. The reliance on unaccrued "future claims" as collateral, without adequate due diligence or robust risk assessment, created a precarious financial structure. This practice effectively packaged and obscured liabilities, masking the underlying credit risk associated with these future receivables.  This mirrors the core issues that plagued Greensill Capital, where the manipulation of invoice data to generate artificial financing appeared to be a key driver of its collapse.  The failure to properly account for the inherent uncertainty and potential for non-payment associated with these future claims represents a fundamental violation of core fund accounting principles, ultimately leading to catastrophic losses for Credit Suisse.

**3. Forensic Synthesis: How Supervisory Failure Allowed Both Breakdowns to Compound into the 2023 Collapse.**

The interconnectedness of these two failures – the liquidity crisis and the asset management debacle – points to a broader systemic issue: supervisory failure. The IMF's assessment of Switzerland's Basel Core Principle grades, highlighting 8 areas of "Materially Non-Compliant" practices, reveals a systemic weakness in the regulatory oversight that allowed both the inadequate LCR calibration and the flawed asset management practices to persist.  This supervisory deficiency created a fertile environment where risk was underestimated, and potential vulnerabilities were overlooked. The combination of insufficient liquidity buffers (as evidenced by the LCR shortcomings) and the opaque, potentially fraudulent nature of the Supply Chain Finance Funds (as exemplified by the Greensill-like practices) created a perfect storm, accelerating the collapse of Credit Suisse.  The failure to proactively identify and address these compounding weaknesses ultimately led to a loss of confidence, triggering the bank's rapid downfall.
